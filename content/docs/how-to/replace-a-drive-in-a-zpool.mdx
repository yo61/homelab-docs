Replacing a pool member is two commands. Everything that makes it take a morning happens
before you get to run them: confirming which physical drive is the failing one, and getting
the new drive to appear at all.

The worked example is `nas1` in September 2026, replacing `8DJ5DESY` — an Ultrastar He12
that had lost its helium seal — in the four-wide `raidz2` pool `space`.

## 1. Check what actually arrived

Three properties of the replacement matter, and all three are readable from the label
before you open the case.

**Interface.** The Ultrastar He12 ships in SATA and SAS variants with nearly identical part
numbers — `HUH721212ALE600` is SATA, `HUH721212AL5200` is SAS. Refurbished stock mixes them
up constantly, because SAS drives are cheaper second-hand and listings conflate the two.

A SAS drive physically cannot seat in a SATA backplane. SATA splits the drive edge into a
15-pin power blade and a 7-pin signal blade with a notch between them; SAS bridges that notch
into one continuous connector to carry its second port. The bridge is the keying feature. A
SATA drive drops into a SAS backplane happily, but never the reverse — so if a drive goes
*almost* all the way in and produces nothing, check the connector for the notch.

**Capacity, in bytes.** `zpool replace` fails if the new device is even marginally smaller
than the partition it replaces. Vendors' "12 TB" are not all the same number:

```bash
echo $(( $(cat /sys/block/sdd/size) * 512 ))
```

On `nas1` every member reads exactly `12000138625024`. Same model or larger is the safe buy.

**Sector format.** A vdev's `ashift` is fixed at creation. If the pool was created at
`ashift=9`, a 4Kn drive cannot serve 512-byte I/O and will not work as a replacement. Check
with `zdb`, which needs root and is the only reading worth trusting:

```bash
sudo zdb -C space | grep -i ashift
```

`space` is `ashift=12`, so 512e and 4Kn are both fine here.

## 2. The 3.3 V power-disable trap

<Callout type="warn">
  This is the one that costs hours. A drive affected by it is invisible to every software
  diagnostic — no block device, no PHY link, nothing in `dmesg` — and behaves identically in
  every bay you try, which makes it look like a dead drive or an uncabled backplane.
</Callout>

SATA revision 3.3 redefined **P3** of the 15-pin power connector. It had been a third 3.3 V
supply pin; it became `PWDIS`, a control input. Held high, the drive stays in reset: it does
not spin up, does not train a link, and does not announce itself. The feature exists so that
datacentre operators can power-cycle one drive through the backplane instead of walking to
the rack.

Many enterprise drives from roughly 2017 onward implement it. Power supplies and backplanes
that predate the change — including the one in `nas1` — still deliver 3.3 V on P1–P3 as
ordinary power. The result is a perfectly healthy drive that never wakes up.

<Callout type="info">
  The reported SATA version is not a screening test. The drive fitted here identifies as
  `SATA Version is: SATA 3.2` — nominally a revision older than the one that introduced
  `PWDIS` — and honours power disable regardless. Vendors shipped the feature ahead of the
  version string catching up, so assume any enterprise drive of this generation is affected
  until proven otherwise.
</Callout>

The fix is to break that one connection. In order of preference:

- A strip of Kapton tape over P3 — third pin from the 3.3 V end of the 15-pin segment.
- A Molex-to-SATA power adapter, which carries no 3.3 V line at all.
- Cutting the orange wire, if the lead is not shared with anything else.

<Callout type="info">
  On `nas1` the fix already existed as an undocumented piece of insulation on the drive that
  came out. Moving it to the replacement made the drive appear immediately — and left the
  old drive unable to power up. If you are keeping the old drive as a fallback during the
  resilver, tape its P3 as well before you shelve it.
</Callout>

## 3. Find a port the drive can actually appear on

Free ports at the controller are not the same as bays you can use. Two separate things have
to be true.

**The bay has to be cabled.** Count live links against disks:

```bash
for l in /sys/class/ata_link/link*; do
  printf '%-8s %s\n' "$(basename "$l")" "$(cat "$l"/sata_spd)"
done | sort -V
```

`nas1` shows eight links for eight disks. Every previous drive swap reused a bay that already
had a drive in it, which proves nothing about the empty ones — a chassis with more bays than
the builder cabled will happily accept a drive into a bay that goes nowhere.

**The port has to be armed for hot insertion.** Read `PxCMD`:

```bash
for h in /sys/class/scsi_host/host*; do
  echo "$(basename "$h") $(cat "$h"/ahci_port_cmd 2>/dev/null)"
done
```

Bit 18 (`0x40000`) is `HPCP`, hot-plug capable. Bits 1 and 2 are `SUD` and `POD` — set when
the port is driving the PHY and looking for a device. On `nas1` the two free 6 Gb/s ports on
the Marvell 88SE9172 read `0x700010`: no `HPCP`, and `SUD`/`POD` clear. That port is
electrically quiet, and a working drive inserted there produces nothing until the controller
is re-initialised. The free Intel C2000 ports read `0x44016` — armed and listening, at
3.0 Gb/s.

For a 7200 rpm drive the speed difference does not matter: sustained transfer is roughly
200–260 MB/s against SATA II's 300 MB/s ceiling, so the resilver takes the same time either
way.

If you do need a Marvell port to re-probe without rebooting, and it has no disks on it:

```bash
echo 1 | sudo tee /sys/bus/pci/devices/0000:04:00.0/remove
echo 1 | sudo tee /sys/bus/pci/rescan
```

## 4. Identify the failing drive physically

`nas1` has no locate LED. There is no SES enclosure (`/sys/class/enclosure` is empty), no
`ledctl`, and `CAP.EMS` reads 0 on all four controllers, so the backplane is passive and
nothing addressable can light a bay. That leaves the activity LED.

First confirm the device letter still maps to the serial you mean. Letters move across
reboots, and position in `zpool status` is vdev creation order, which says nothing about
device naming:

```bash
for u in $(zpool status space | awk '/-.*-.*-.*-/ {print $1}'); do
  p=$(readlink -f /dev/disk/by-partuuid/$u)
  printf '%s  %s  %s\n' "$u" "$p" "$(lsblk -dno SERIAL /dev/$(lsblk -no PKNAME $p))"
done
```

Then pulse the drive and watch for the bay that follows the rhythm:

```bash
# READ-ONLY. Ctrl-C to stop.
while true; do
  timeout 5 dd if=/dev/sdd of=/dev/null bs=1M iflag=direct status=none
  sleep 5
done
```

<Callout type="warn">
  Check the direction of that `dd` before running it. `if=` the disk, `of=/dev/null`.
  Reversed, it overwrites a live pool member.
</Callout>

`iflag=direct` is doing real work. Without `O_DIRECT` the second pass onward is served from
the page cache, so the LED goes dark while `dd` still reports throughput.

The pulsing matters too. A live pool gives every member background flicker, so "which one is
busy" is a much weaker signal than "which one matches the beat".

## 5. Take it offline before pulling

```bash
sudo zpool offline space ed5879e1-e883-441d-84a2-b2c919dd99b8
sudo zpool status space          # expect DEGRADED, that member OFFLINE
```

Offlining first marks the device unavailable in the label before any I/O is issued to it, so
the vdev transitions cleanly. Pulling a live member instead means ZFS discovers it through
failed I/O — outstanding writes error, the vdev passes through `FAULTED`, and the error
counters fill up for no diagnostic benefit.

It also makes the replace unambiguous. Replacing an `OFFLINE` member tells ZFS the old device
is intentionally gone; replacing one that is half-present invites reads from a drive whose
failure mode is long timeouts rather than clean errors.

Read the label as the drive comes out. It is the only check that cannot be fooled by a
mistaken bay map. If the serial is wrong, put it back and `zpool online` before touching
anything else.

## 6. Confirm the new drive enumerated

```bash
lsblk -dno NAME,SIZE,MODEL,SERIAL,TRAN | grep -v '^zd'
cat /sys/class/ata_link/link9/sata_spd
```

Two sysfs classes answer different questions, and keeping them apart saves a lot of guessing:

| path | question | meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `ata_link/linkN/sata_spd` | did the PHY train? | a speed means the cable and drive electronics are alive |
| `ata_device/devN.0/class` | did it answer IDENTIFY? | `ata` is a disk, `atapi` is packet-command, `unknown` is no response |

A speed with `class=unknown` is a drive that is present and failing to identify — suspect the
drive or a marginal cable. No speed at all means the PHY never came up, which no amount of
rescanning will fix: that is power, cabling, or `PWDIS`.

<Callout type="info">
  `echo "- - -" > /sys/class/scsi_host/hostN/scan` does not help a SATA drive that has not
  linked. It walks the SCSI *target* space behind a transport that is already up, which is
  meaningful for SAS expanders and iSCSI. SATA has one device per port and no target
  enumeration, so with no link there is nothing to find — the rescan reports success and
  changes nothing.
</Callout>

## 7. Check SMART before it joins the pool

```bash
sudo smartctl -i /dev/sdd
sudo smartctl -H /dev/sdd
sudo smartctl -A /dev/sdd
sudo smartctl -l selftest /dev/sdd
```

Read the **normalised** `VALUE` against `THRESH`, not `RAW_VALUE`. OEM-badged drives are
usually absent from smartctl's database — `MB012000GWDFE` is HPE's part number for this same
He12 platform — and without a database entry smartctl applies a default raw format, so
`RAW_VALUE` is uninterpretable. The normalised value is computed by the drive's own firmware
and is what its overall-health verdict is based on.

For helium drives, **attribute 22 is the helium level**, and it is the attribute that
condemned the drive being replaced here at `001/025`. A healthy drive reads `100/025`.

Note what the attribute table does *not* contain. The HPE firmware on this drive omits 197
(pending sectors), 198 (uncorrectable) and 199 (UDMA CRC), so those cannot be checked at all.
Absence of an attribute is not a pass.

<Callout type="warn">
  A refurbished drive reporting `Power_On_Hours` of 0 has either genuinely never run or has
  had its counters reset by the refurbisher, and the attributes alone cannot distinguish
  those. Cross-check against the device statistics log, which lives in a different log page
  and is not always reset alongside: `sudo smartctl -x /dev/sdd` and read *Power-on Hours*,
  *Head Flying Hours* and *Lifetime Power-On Resets* under **Device Statistics**.
</Callout>

The long self-test (`smartctl -t long`) takes 15+ hours on a 12 TB drive. Worth running if
the new drive is fitted alongside the failing one; not worth it when the pool is sitting
degraded waiting for it, since the resilver writes the whole device anyway and surfaces bad
sectors as resilver errors.

## 8. Replace and verify

Always address the new drive by `/dev/disk/by-id/`, never by letter:

```bash
sudo zpool replace space ed5879e1-e883-441d-84a2-b2c919dd99b8 \
  /dev/disk/by-id/ata-MB012000GWDFE_8HKUX68H
sudo zpool status space
```

The pool stays `DEGRADED` until the resilver completes — roughly a day for 12 TB. When it
finishes, the member's partuuid changes, so any notes recording the old one are stale.
Re-derive the map rather than trusting what you wrote down:

```bash
for u in $(zpool status space | awk '/-.*-.*-.*-/ {print $1}'); do
  p=$(readlink -f /dev/disk/by-partuuid/$u)
  printf '%s  %s  %s\n' "$u" "$p" "$(lsblk -dno SERIAL /dev/$(lsblk -no PKNAME $p))"
done
```

Then scrub, and keep the old drive on the shelf until that scrub comes back clean.
